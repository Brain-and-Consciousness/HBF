#### Title: "Why do we disagree about consciousness, and does it matter? The psychological origins and consequences of disagreement about consciousness." [[Slides]()]

#### Abstract:
Why do smart, informed people disagree so profoundly about consciousness? It is striking how deeply researchers can disagree after decades of scientific and philosophical research; for example, competing theories often draw on much of the same evidence yet arrive at fundamentally different conclusions about what consciousness is and how it arises. This disagreement matters: Beliefs about what entities are conscious shape how we think about human embryos and foetuses, non-human animals, patients with disorders of consciousness, and increasingly, artificial intelligence. Such theories do not remain confined to academic debate — policymakers and institutions draw on scientific and philosophical accounts of consciousness when confronting questions of rights, legal protection, healthcare, and the regulation of emerging technologies. Greater agreement is therefore important because different conceptions of consciousness can lead to different conclusions about which beings or systems are conscious and what moral or legal consideration they warrant.

I will present a project — under development — which approaches this disagreement as a psychological phenomenon. The project is an example of what is often called "experimental philosophy". We ask whether broad differences in consciousness-positions are partly explained by stable individual differences in cognitive traits, explanatory styles, and other psychological tendencies (e.g., whether a tendency to blur distinctions between mental and physical phenomena is associated with a person’s position on the “hard problem of consciousness”). Across three studies, the project currently aims to integrate the existing literature, identify and model psychological predictors of consciousness-positions, and investigate societal attitudes that may be influenced by these positions (for example, whether certain consciousness-positions are associated with public attitudes on topics such as the regulation of potentially conscious AI systems). The project will also begin to experimentally investigate the psychological mechanisms underlying key findings on individual differences, while testing a complementary hypothesis that consciousness-positions can be substantially influenced by scientific and philosophical education.

By explaining why researchers can arrive at such divergent positions, and by identifying conditions that may support more constructive scientific dialogue, the project aims to improve our understanding of disagreement in consciousness research and thereby hopefully contribute to more informed ethical and policy decisions.

The project is still at an early stage, and we are currently applying for PhD funding to conduct the research work. At the meeting, in addition to presenting the theoretical background to the project, we therefore hope to get some constructively critical interdisciplinary feedback from HBF colleagues on how the project's research questions and design can be further developed and improved.
